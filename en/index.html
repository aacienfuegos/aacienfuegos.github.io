03

Home automation

Two homes, separate network

Two homes automated and run from the same place: lights, plugs, climate and the whole living room. It answers to voice, to the phone and to the wall panel, but what you notice most is what nobody has to touch — schedule-driven routines, things that fire when you get home, and sensors that start the heating or ping you without anyone asking. 03

Homelab

This is where I tinker. I build things to understand them from the inside, break them and bring them back up, which is where the learning happens: what drags what down when something fails, and in what order. What works stays running; what does not, gets written down so I do not trip twice.

  • 6years tinkering
  • 2nodes in two cities
  • 9virtualised machines
  • 18services running
  • 5network segments

Principles

  • Environment separation What the house uses every day does not live where I experiment. Between the disposable and the things that cannot go down there is a border, and it is only crossed once something has proved it holds.
  • Resource efficiency The hardware is second-hand and runs 365 days a year, so every service is measured in RAM and in watts. Anything that wants 16 GB for what another does with half a gig doesn't make the cut.
  • Deny by default The answer is no until someone says otherwise, and not only on the network: a new service talks to nothing and publishes nothing until it is given permission.
  • Least privilege When the answer is yes, it is exactly what is needed and no more: no token writes where it should not, not one API permission more than needed.
  • Defence in depth From the tunnel to SSO, and from filtering to detection: no single layer is the only thing between the outside and a service.
  • Observability A service does not count as deployed until something watches it and knows how to reach me.
  • Change management Everything that gets touched goes to the changelog with the alternatives I turned down, and nothing gets automated without written stop criteria and a way back.
  • AI across the board AI is part of the day to day: it designs the changes, diagnoses the faults and keeps the documentation current. Always with human oversight — what it proposes is tested in the lab, isolated from production, and goes through me before it touches anything that matters.

On the way

What comes next. None of this is deployed yet.

  • Immutable backups Applying the 3-2-1-1-0 rule: copies that survive an attacker with full control of the machine, impossible to delete for months and verified by actually restoring them.
  • High availability A UPS so a power cut does not take a node down, and new nodes in a quorum cluster so losing one machine does not take DNS, authentication and the applications with it.
  • External surface monitoring Certificate Transparency to find out if a certificate or a subdomain in my name shows up that I did not issue.
  • Automated response Detecting an attack should trigger containment on its own — isolate, block, revoke — instead of ending in a notification someone has to read.
  • Advanced monitoring Going from knowing a service is down to knowing how it behaves: per-service metrics with history, and failures that arrive already investigated by an agent with a proposed fix.
  • Proactive agents The system should not wait for me to ask: it should spot what is needed, build it, deploy it to the lab and leave it tested, waiting for approval.
